apu_switch — two S-DSP programs, swapped at runtime

Two SPC700 programs written straight against the S-DSP, and the way to replace the running one at any time. Press A for a drum kit made of nothing but the DSP's noise generator, B for a cello sung by hardware vibrato — the kit cuts, the cello blooms, and back, forever. The backdrop tracks the active program (red = drums, amber = cello).

The two programs are ports of krom (Peter Lemon)'s PlayNoise and PitchMod demos; the swap is his PlayTwoSong protocol, productized. krom's original swap demo uses samples ripped from commercial games (Axelay, Final Fantasy VI, the Halken logo), so this one swaps between its own two programs instead — same protocol, no rights issues.

ROM mode: LoROM (project default).

Controls

Button Program
A the drum kit (program A, at boot)
B the vibrato cello (program B)

SNES Concepts

  • Raw APU programming via the apu module (IPL upload from C)
  • Cooperative APU reset: CPU→APU messaging over $2140 after boot
  • The IPL ROM is re-entrant — re-enable it (CONTROL bit 7), jump to $FFC0, and the $AA/$BB + $CC upload protocol runs again
  • The S-DSP noise source (NON, the FLG noise clock) as an instrument; the ADSR envelope is the drum type
  • Pitch modulation (PMON): voice N−1's output scales voice N's pitch; a silent voice as a hardware LFO
  • FIR echo configuration (ESA/EDL/EFB/FIR0) and clearing the echo buffer RAM before enabling writes

The Swap

The apu module's upload path runs once, at boot. apuReset() adds the missing capability: it writes APU_RESET_MAGIC to I/O port 0; each SPC700 program polls for it inside its wait loops (the APU_CHECK_RESET idiom from templates/memmap_spc700.inc), silences the DSP, acks on its output ports, and jumps back to the IPL boot ROM at $FFC0 — which is re-entrant, so the full boot handshake works again and the next apuUpload() / apuExecute() proceeds exactly like the first.

Hot-swap hygiene. The DSP arrives dirty in the second program: this example's cello inherited the hi-hat's ADSR on its LFO voice and lost its vibrato until the program zeroed V0ADSR explicitly. Every register a voice depends on must be written — never rely on reset defaults after a swap. The tests check it: after B, the noise voice (NON) and the noise clock (FLG) of the drum kit are back to 0.

Program A — a Drum Kit with Zero Samples

The S-DSP has a built-in white-noise generator: set a voice's NON flag and the voice plays noise instead of BRR data. Every drum in the looping bar — kick 480 ms, closed hi-hat 240 ms, open hi-hat 240 ms, snare 960 ms — is nothing but three register writes: the noise clock (FLG bits 0-4: low = rumble, high = hiss), an ADSR envelope (fast one-shot = closed hat, long release = open hat), and a bare KON. A single-tap FIR echo (feedback 80) gives the kit its room. The program ships zero bytes of sample data.

Measured against krom's original (luna --audio-out, 40 s captures): onset gap patterns identical to 0.01 s, per-hit spectral centroids within 2 %, RMS within 1 %.

Program B — Hardware Vibrato by Pitch Modulation

PMON multiplies a voice's pitch by the output of the previous voice, every sample, in hardware. Voice 1 holds a looping cello note at C5; voice 0 plays a 9-byte square-wave BRR block at pitch $0003 — far below audibility, it is a slow LFO whose tiny amplitude (GAIN = 2) sets the vibrato depth. The cello enters dry; one second in, the LFO keys on and the note starts to sing. Each note rings out like a bow stroke (instant attack, ~7 s fade) and is re-bowed every 7 s. A maximum-length FIR echo (EDL 15, feedback 100) does the concert hall.

Measured against the original: pitch 174.4 Hz vs 174.3 Hz, vibrato depth ±0.82 % on both, LFO rate ~5 Hz on both.

Why the envelope differs from the original. krom's demo sets a flat GAIN 127 — which would sustain the note forever — yet it audibly decays. His SPC700 program has no final idle loop: after the last KON, execution falls off the end of the code, through the sample directory, and runs the cello's BRR bytes as opcodes (luna's spc-dump: his PC ends up at $0548, inside the sample data). The decay is that runaway code scribbling on the DSP. This port produces the bow stroke on purpose: the cello's filename in krom's repo encodes its envelope (ADSR $FF/$E8), so the program sets that ADSR, parks in a clean idle loop, and re-keys the voice every 7 s.

Sample Provenance

res/cello.brr is synthesized here (devtools/audio_assets/build.py): a bow's scratch settling into an exactly periodic string tone, 100 samples a period at 17 463 Hz — the rate krom's pitch $08BB ($8BB0 >> 4) plays a sample at — so the note is the F3 (174.6 Hz) the program was written for. It loops on its last 8 periods, from byte 2457. It replaced krom's own cello, whose repository carries no licence.

Validation

luna manifests: apu_switch.toml (boot state, non-silent drums, the swap asserted on current_song), audio_apu_switch_noise_kick.toml / _noise_snare.toml (the drum kit's DSP registers, kick then snare) and audio_apu_switch_pitch_mod_dry.toml / _vibrato.toml (the cello before and after the LFO keys on, and nothing left of the drums).
