Screenshot

A square of the Rue basse seen from above, 512 × 448: seventeen data shards drift over the paving, six residents — Kit, a frog, an owl, a pangolin, a raccoon and a rat — pace up and down round the neon ring at its centre, and the D-pad scrolls a 256 × 224 view of it (BG1, a 64 × 64 map, scrolls with the camera). Every frame the example hands its world-space arrays to oamPlaceWorld(), once per batch: the call subtracts the camera, hides what is off screen, writes the OAM entries and the ninth x bit, and reports in visible[] which sprites it placed — the loop a scrolling game runs every frame, in assembly. The residents are sorted by depth and the batch's order says which resident each slot shows, the way a game seen from above sorts its actors; each faces the way it walks (its back turned going up).

SNES Concepts

  • World coordinates minus a camera give screen coordinates; a sprite partly off the left edge needs the ninth x bit (x in 256..511), which the PPU reads as negative.
  • OAM culling: a sprite off screen is hidden (x = 257, y = 240), not left where it was.
  • OAM order is drawing order: the lower slot is in front. Seen from above, the sprite lower on the screen is nearer, so the residents are sorted by y every frame (an insertion sort of six bytes) and order permutes the slots — the arrays stay indexed by the resident, nothing is copied.
  • Structure of arrays (one array per property) is what the 65816 indexes cheaply; an OamWorldBatch is filled with their addresses once.
  • A batch has one size: the shards are the SMALL size of OBJ_SIZE16_L32 (16 × 16), the residents the LARGE one (32 × 32), set once with oamSetSize(): the call never touches the size bit.
  • A sprite's frame is its entry of the batch's tile array: a resident walking up gets its north frame, one walking down its south frame, and the call writes whichever is there. Each resident has its OBJ palette (1 to 6, in its attribute); the shards share palette 0.
  • visible[] tells the game which sprites were placed — here it counts the shards in view; a game reads it to stream the animation frames of the sprites that are shown and skip the others, so that a sprite off screen costs no VRAM DMA.
  • A batch costs the same whether its sprites are on screen or not (the call tests each one); a game that hides a whole group skips the call with a flag of its own rather than placing hidden sprites.

VRAM Layout

Region Content

$4000 OBJ tiles: twelve frames of the residents, then the shard (tile 192)
